1. The outer retina: four layers that decide everything

Study notes for coursework. Not clinical guidance.

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a disease of one small patch of retina and of the four layers stacked beneath it. Nearly every result in an AMD paper, human or fish, is a claim about one of those layers, so learn the stack before anything else: photoreceptors, the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), Bruch's membrane and the choriocapillaris.

1.1 The macula and fovea

The macula is the central patch of the human retina, roughly 5 to 6 mm across, yellow because it concentrates the carotenoids lutein and zeaxanthin. At its centre the fovea holds almost nothing but cones, packed at the highest density anywhere in the eye, with the inner retinal layers swept aside so light reaches them directly. Rods peak in a ring just outside the fovea. This is the tissue used for reading, faces and fine detail, which is why losing a few square millimetres of it is disabling while the peripheral field stays intact.

Only primates, and some birds and reptiles, have a true macula. Mice and rats are nocturnal and rod-dominated with no fovea at all, which is the single biggest reason AMD has resisted modelling. Hold on to that fact: it is the argument every fish paper opens with.

1.2 Photoreceptors

Rods and cones each have an outer segment, a stack of membrane discs loaded with opsin, joined by a narrow connecting cilium to an inner segment packed with mitochondria. Outer segments are rebuilt continuously: new discs are added at the base and the oldest tips are shed every day on a circadian schedule. Photoreceptors have among the highest metabolic rates of any cell in the body, so they depend completely on the blood supply below and on the RPE between.

Why the inner and outer segment length matters. If outer segments shorten but keep their width, material is being lost. If they widen instead, the measurement has changed without material loss. Papers measure both for exactly this reason, and the distinction is what turns "shorter segments" into evidence of degeneration.

1.3 The retinal pigment epithelium

The RPE is a single layer of pigmented, hexagonal, non-dividing cells. One RPE cell serves dozens of photoreceptors for an entire lifetime, which makes it the textbook post-mitotic cell: damage accumulates with nothing to dilute it. Its jobs are worth listing, because each is a failure point.

1.4 Bruch's membrane and the choriocapillaris

Bruch's membrane is a thin five-layered sheet between the RPE and the choroid: the RPE basement membrane, inner collagenous layer, elastic layer, outer collagenous layer, and the choriocapillaris basement membrane. Everything the RPE exchanges with blood crosses it. With age it thickens, calcifies and fills with lipid, especially esterified cholesterol in lipoprotein-like particles, which slows transport the way a clogging filter does. The choriocapillaris is a dense sheet of wide fenestrated capillaries supplying the photoreceptors; it thins and drops out with age and further in AMD.

Treat the outer retina as a kitchen. Photoreceptors are the cooks making scraps, the RPE is the dishwasher, Bruch's membrane is the drain and the choroid is the sewer. Drusen are grease in the drain; subretinal drusenoid deposits are scraps left on the counter.

One more structural idea you need for the fish papers. The human retina is built once and then only ages. The fish retina keeps growing at its rim, in a ring of stem cells called the ciliary marginal zone, so the centre of a fish eye is the oldest tissue and the edge is the youngest. That means a centre-versus-periphery comparison inside a single fish eye is partly an age comparison, and papers use it as an internal control.

2. The disease: deposits, stages, two late forms

AMD is the leading cause of irreversible central vision loss in older people in high-income countries, affecting on the order of 200 million people worldwide with projections approaching 300 million by 2040 as populations age. Peripheral vision survives, so even advanced disease is not total blindness, but reading, driving and face recognition go.

2.1 Deposits: the two kinds, and why the difference matters

Drusen are extracellular deposits between the RPE basal lamina and the inner collagenous layer of Bruch's membrane, so below the RPE. They contain esterified and unesterified cholesterol, phospholipids, oxidised phospholipids, apolipoproteins such as APOE, complement components and regulators, amyloid beta, vitronectin and crystallins. Small hard drusen under 63 µm are ordinary ageing. Medium (63 to 125 µm) and large (over 125 µm) soft drusen mark disease and predict progression.

Subretinal drusenoid deposits (SDD, also called reticular pseudodrusen) sit on the other side of the RPE, between the RPE and the photoreceptor outer segments, so above the RPE. They are granular, contain unesterified cholesterol, are less lipid-rich than drusen, and cluster in rod-rich retina outside the fovea. They carry a high risk of progressing to late disease, geographic atrophy especially. Spaide, Ooto and Curcio (2018) is the standard reference.

Keep this straight and half the fish literature becomes readable. Drusen: below the RPE, lipid-rich. SDD: above the RPE, lipid-containing but not lipid-rich. A deposit's position says which side of the pipeline failed; its composition says what the material is. When a fish develops a deposit that is lipid-rich (drusen-like composition) but sits above the RPE (SDD-like position), the two criteria disagree, and that is exactly the result the killifish paper has to argue about.

2.3 Geographic atrophy

Geographic atrophy is the death of RPE cells together with the photoreceptors that depend on them and the choriocapillaris beneath. Lesions often begin around the fovea and spare it at first, then enlarge and merge at roughly 1.5 to 2 mm² per year. The agreed imaging definition is complete RPE and outer retinal atrophy. Somewhere around 10 to 20 per cent of people with AMD reach this stage, and until 2023 nothing could be offered at all.

2.4 Neovascular AMD

Here new vessels grow where they do not belong, driven chiefly by VEGF-A. The current term is macular neovascularisation, typed by position:

  • Type 1: from the choroid into the space under the RPE. The most common; polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy is a variant more frequent in Asian populations.
  • Type 2: through the RPE into the subretinal space.
  • Type 3: begins in the deep retinal capillaries and grows downward.

Leaking fluid and blood distort the photoreceptors; untreated, the lesion ends as a fibrous disciform scar. This form is only around 10 to 15 per cent of cases but causes most of the severe vision loss.

2.5 Symptoms and how it is measured

Patients notice metamorphopsia, in which straight lines look wavy, a central blur or blind spot, poor contrast, and slow dark adaptation, which is often the earliest complaint of all.

Learn the human method alongside its fish counterpart, because the translation between them is where papers earn or lose credibility.

3. Mechanisms: six strands, one web

No single cause explains AMD. Think of several stressors converging on the RPE and Bruch's membrane, with genotype setting how well each person's tissue copes. Fish papers almost always model one strand, so knowing the strands lets you place any paper immediately.

  • ageing and oxidative stress
  • RPE overloaded
  • lipid and debris build-up
  • complement and immune activation
  • RPE and choriocapillaris loss
  • atrophy or neovascularisation

3.1 Oxidative stress

The macula combines high oxygen tension from the choroid, lifelong light exposure, and membranes rich in the polyunsaturated fatty acid DHA, which oxidises readily. Lipid peroxidation generates oxidation-specific epitopes such as carboxyethylpyrrole and oxidised phosphatidylcholine. These are found in drusen and are read by innate immunity as danger signals, which is how an oxidative problem becomes an inflammatory one. Handa and colleagues (2017) is the reference review, and it is the paper the fish deposit-staining work leans on.

3.2 Lipofuscin and the visual cycle

Each RPE cell digests millions of outer-segment discs across a lifetime. Some retinoid by-products are indigestible, most famously A2E, a bis-retinoid formed from two molecules of all-trans retinal. They collect in lysosomes as lipofuscin, which is autofluorescent (the basis of fundus autofluorescence imaging), photosensitising, and disruptive to lysosomal function. Stargardt disease, from ABCA4 mutations, is the accelerated monogenic version and is often used to model this strand.

3.3 Lipids and Bruch's membrane

The RPE packages lipid, both from phagocytosed outer segments and from plasma lipoproteins, into APOB-containing particles and secretes it basally. With age these particles accumulate in Bruch's membrane as a lipid wall, then condense into basal linear deposits and soft drusen. Several risk genes (APOE, CETP, LIPC, ABCA1) are lipid genes, and drusen resemble the lipid cores of atherosclerotic plaques, though statins have not proven useful as treatment.

3.4 Complement and inflammation

Complement is part of innate immunity. C3 is cleaved into C3a and C3b; C3b amplifies the alternative pathway; C5 cleavage yields inflammatory C5a and the membrane attack complex C5b-9. The regulator complement factor H normally switches the alternative pathway off on host surfaces by binding glycosaminoglycans and oxidised lipids. The CFH Y402H variant binds these less effectively, so complement runs hotter on Bruch's membrane. Drusen contain complement components, and the first two approved atrophy drugs target C3 and C5, which is about as direct a confirmation of a mechanism as clinical medicine offers.

Inflammation is also cellular. The healthy subretinal space contains almost no immune cells. In AMD, microglia and blood-derived macrophages enter it, gather at drusen and atrophy borders, and may either clear debris or damage photoreceptors. Where macrophages go is one of the main readouts in the fish papers, precisely because it is cheap to stain and unambiguous to count.

3.5 Mitochondria and metabolic competition

Aged and AMD RPE shows mitochondrial DNA damage, fewer and structurally abnormal mitochondria, and a metabolic shift. The current framing is competition: a healthy RPE burns fatty acids and lactate and passes glucose up to the photoreceptors, while a stressed RPE that keeps the glucose starves the cells above it. Stressed photoreceptors in turn send down more lipid to process. Kanow and colleagues (2017, eLife) established this ecosystem view and is heavily cited for it.

3.6 Angiogenesis

When the RPE is stressed or the choroid hypoxic, the VEGF-to-PEDF balance shifts, Bruch's membrane is breached by inflammation and matrix proteases, and choroidal vessels invade. VEGF-A also makes vessels leaky, producing the fluid seen on imaging. Angiopoietin-2 destabilises vessels and sensitises them to VEGF, which is the rationale for the bispecific drug faricimab.

Synthesis worth memorising. Atrophy is mainly the RPE-degeneration and complement strands. Neovascular disease is mainly the VEGF strand laid on the same background. They are two endpoints of one process, and many eyes eventually show both.

4. Risk, genetics and what treatment exists

Age dominates everything: prevalence climbs steeply after 60. Genome-wide association work by the International AMD Genomics Consortium (Fritsche et al. 2016) identified 52 independent variants at 34 loci, which between them explain an unusually large share of heritability for a complex disease.

The two big loci are "one and ten". Chromosome 1 is complement, through CFH. Chromosome 10 is ARMS2/HTRA1. Between them sits most of the genetic risk.

4.1 The teleost genome problem

Ray-finned fish went through an extra whole-genome duplication, so many human genes have two zebrafish or killifish copies labelled a and b: rpe65a, apoeb, htra1b, rlbp1a. Three consequences you must apply when reading any fish knockout:

  • Knocking out one paralogue may show nothing, because the other compensates. A negative result is weak evidence.
  • The two copies may have split the original job between tissues (subfunctionalisation), so a fish paralogue may do only part of what the human gene does. Fleisch and colleagues (2008) showed exactly this for a retinoid-binding protein in the zebrafish cone visual cycle.
  • The naming of the human disease gene does not guarantee the fish copy is the right one to test.

4.2 Supplements

The Age-Related Eye Disease Study (2001) found that high-dose antioxidants with zinc cut the five-year risk of progressing to late disease by roughly a quarter in people with intermediate disease or late disease in one eye. AREDS2 (2013) swapped beta-carotene, which raised lung cancer risk in smokers, for lutein and zeaxanthin. The AREDS formulation does not help early disease and does not prevent the disease, so it belongs to the same late-acting category as everything else below.

4.3 Anti-VEGF for neovascular disease

Intravitreal anti-VEGF turned neovascular AMD from near-certain severe vision loss into a manageable condition: in the pivotal trials most patients retained vision and about a third gained substantially. The cost is repeated injections, every 4 to 16 weeks, often indefinitely.

4.4 Complement inhibitors for atrophy

In 2023 two drugs for geographic atrophy were approved in the United States, both given by repeated intravitreal injection: pegcetacoplan, which inhibits C3, and avacincaptad pegol, which inhibits C5. Each slows lesion growth by roughly 15 to 20 per cent, but neither demonstrated preserved visual function in the pivotal trials, and both increase conversion to neovascular disease. European regulators declined pegcetacoplan on that basis. Treat them as proof that the complement strand is real, not as a solved problem.

The gap the fish work aims at. Every approved therapy acts late: on vessels, or on complement once atrophy is under way. Nothing clears deposits, restores RPE, or blocks the early-to-late transition. A model that forms deposits spontaneously, in months, is aimed squarely at that gap.

5. Why model this in fish

Every AMD model compromises. Judging a fish paper means knowing what it is competing against.

ModelStrengthsWeaknesses
Non-human primatesA true macula; some develop drusen with ageExpensive, slow, small numbers, ethical constraints
Mouse genetic modelsGenetics and tools, abundantNo macula, few cones, nocturnal; phenotypes mild and variable
Laser-induced neovascularisationReliable and quantitative for drug testingAn injury model, not ageing; no deposits
Human iPSC-RPE and organoidsHuman genetics, patient-specific linesNo choroid, no blood, no immune system, no ageing
ZebrafishCone-rich, diurnal, fast genetics, transparent larvae, drug screening at scaleLives 3 to 5 years, regenerates retina and RPE, no macula
Turquoise killifishAges naturally in months, cone-rich, forms deposits spontaneouslyNewer model with fewer tools; no macula; key data still recent or unreviewed

5.1 What fish share with the human macula

Zebrafish and killifish are diurnal, so their retinas are cone-rich, much closer in photoreceptor composition to the human macula than any mouse retina. Zebrafish have four cone types in a regular mosaic, plus rods. The layering of neural retina, RPE, Bruch's membrane and choroid is conserved, as are visual cycle genes and most known AMD risk genes. The cone-specific visual cycle, reviewed by Wang and Kefalov (2011), is in fact better studied in fish than in mammals, because a cone-dominant retina makes it tractable.

5.2 What fish do differently, and how papers handle it

  • Regeneration. Fish Müller glia re-enter the cell cycle and rebuild lost neurons, and fish RPE regenerates. A gift for regeneration research, a confound for degeneration research, because loss can be masked by replacement. Lahne and colleagues (2020) review the mechanism.
  • Lifelong growth. The ciliary marginal zone keeps adding retina at the rim, giving the centre-versus-periphery age gradient described in section 1.
  • Duplicated genes. Paralogues complicate every knockout, as in section 4.1.
  • No macula. Any claim about "macular" pathology in a fish is an analogy to cone-rich retina, never anatomy. Watch for papers that blur this.
  • Ectothermy and a different immune system. Complement genes exist but are expanded and not one-to-one with human ones, so complement findings transfer least well of all.
Zebrafish are the genetics workhorse, killifish are the clock. Zebrafish answer "what does this gene do?". Killifish answer "what does time do?".

6. The papers

Seven papers, in the order that builds understanding: the human anchor, the oxidised-lipid mechanism, two zebrafish mutants, the killifish ageing groundwork, the killifish transcriptome, and the killifish AMD paper that ties them together. For each one: the question, the method, the result, why it matters, and what it cannot show.

6.1 Human anchor: what a deposit actually is

Spaide RF, Ooto S, Curcio CA (2018). Subretinal drusenoid deposits, also known as pseudodrusen. Survey of Ophthalmology.

Question. What are these deposits that imaging shows above the RPE, how do they differ from drusen, and what do they predict?

What it establishes. Two separate deposit types with different positions, compositions, retinal distributions and risks. Drusen: below the RPE, lipid-rich, concentrated in cone-rich central retina. Subretinal drusenoid deposits: above the RPE, lipid-containing but not lipid-rich, concentrated in rod-rich retina, strongly predictive of progression to atrophy.

Why read it first. Every fish deposit claim is scored against these criteria. Without them, "the fish has deposits" is unfalsifiable; with them, you can ask the sharp question: which kind, by position, by composition, and do the two answers agree?

Limits. A review of human imaging and donor histology, so it describes but cannot test mechanism. It also cannot say how a deposit forms in the first place, because you cannot watch it happen in a person.

6.2 Mechanism: oxidised lipid as the bridge from ageing to inflammation

Handa JT, Cano M, Wang L, Datta S, Liu T (2017). Lipids, oxidized lipids, oxidation-specific epitopes, and age-related macular degeneration. Biochimica et Biophysica Acta.

Question. How does lipid accumulation in the outer retina turn into the inflammation and cell loss seen in AMD?

What it establishes. Oxidised lipids are not inert waste. Lipid peroxidation produces oxidation-specific epitopes that innate immunity recognises as danger signals, activating complement and recruiting immune cells. Drusen contain them. This is the mechanistic join between the oxidative strand and the complement strand of section 3.

Why it matters for the fish work. It licenses a specific experiment: stain a deposit for oxidised phospholipids. If the stain is positive, the deposit is not merely fat, it is immunologically active material. Both fish papers below use exactly this logic, which is why their oxidised-phospholipid panels matter more than the plain lipid stains.

Limits. Largely correlative in humans; association of an epitope with a lesion is not proof it caused the lesion.

6.3 Zebrafish: a photoreceptor defect alone can make deposits

Noel NCL, Nadolski NJ, Hocking JC, MacDonald IM, Allison WT (2020). Progressive photoreceptor dysfunction and age-related macular degeneration-like features in rp1l1 mutant zebrafish. Cells 9:2214.

Question. Can a zebrafish mutant reproduce AMD-like subretinal deposits? RP1L1 is a photoreceptor ciliary protein that helps organise outer-segment discs; human RP1L1 mutations cause occult macular dystrophy and retinitis pigmentosa.

Approach. CRISPR/Cas9 knockout of rp1l1, followed across life with histology, electron microscopy, lipid staining and visual function testing.

Findings. Mutants showed disorganised outer segments and progressive photoreceptor dysfunction, and developed lipid-rich deposits between the photoreceptors and the RPE, interpreted as subretinal drusenoid deposits. Reported as the first zebrafish model of photoreceptor degeneration with such deposits. Follow-up work found the deposits enlarged with age, and that occasional deposits appeared even in old wild-type fish.

Why it matters. It shifts the causal arrow. The intuitive story is that a failing RPE lets debris pile up. Here the RPE is genetically intact and the photoreceptor is broken, and deposits form anyway, because material the RPE cannot cope with arrives from above. So deposits on the apical side can be a photoreceptor-origin problem, which is a real argument about how human subretinal drusenoid deposits arise.

Limits. A monogenic, early-onset disease gene, not ageing. Deposits are subretinal, not sub-RPE drusen. Complement and the choroid are not the subject. And zebrafish regenerate, so degeneration severity is damped.

6.4 Zebrafish again: one visual cycle gene, two copies, two different cells

Schlegel DK, Ramkumar S, von Lintig J, Neuhauss SCF (2021). Disturbed retinoid metabolism upon loss of rlbp1a impairs cone function and leads to subretinal lipid deposits and photoreceptor degeneration in the zebrafish retina. eLife 10:e71473.

Question. RLBP1 encodes CRALBP, a 36 kDa soluble retinoid carrier in the visual cycle. Human mutations cause a spread of recessive blinding conditions: Bothnia dystrophy, retinitis pigmentosa, retinitis punctata albescens, fundus albipunctatus and Newfoundland rod-cone dystrophy. Why one gene produces so many phenotypes was not understood. Can a zebrafish model explain it?

Approach, and why the design is elegant. Zebrafish carry two copies of the gene, and crucially they are expressed in different cells: rlbp1a in the RPE, rlbp1b in Müller glia. That accident of duplication hands you a cell-type-specific knockout for free, with no conditional genetics. The authors made single and double mutants and tested each.

Findings. Only rlbp1a, the RPE copy, mattered for vision. Those mutants had reduced chromophore, attenuated cone responses to light, and accumulated 11-cis and all-trans retinyl esters that appeared as enlarged lipid droplets in the RPE, resembling the subretinal yellow-white lesions seen in patients. With age they developed retinal thinning and both cone and rod dystrophy. The rlbp1b Müller glia mutants had no visual impairment, and the double mutant simply reproduced the rlbp1a phenotype.

Why it matters here. Two reasons. First, it is the worked example of the paralogue problem from section 4.1: had the authors knocked out only rlbp1b, they would have concluded the gene was dispensable for vision, which is exactly the false negative to watch for. The duplication split the ancestral job between two cell types, and only one of them carries the visual load. Second, it gives a different route to lipid accumulation from Noel 2020. Here nothing is wrong with the photoreceptor or with phagocytosis; a metabolic blockage in the visual cycle backs retinoid up inside the RPE until it precipitates as droplets. Same end result, different cause, which is why "the fish has lipid deposits" means nothing until you say where and from what.

Limits. A monogenic disease gene again, not ageing. The droplets sit inside RPE cells rather than extracellularly in Bruch's membrane, so they are not drusen in the human sense. And the human conditions it models are inherited dystrophies, not AMD, so the relevance is mechanistic rather than direct.

6.5 Killifish groundwork: establishing that the eye ages at all

Vanhunsel S, Bergmans S, Beckers A, et al. (2021). The killifish visual system as an in vivo model to study brain aging and rejuvenation. npj Aging and Mechanisms of Disease. DOI 10.1038/s41514-021-00077-4.

Question. Does the visual system of the turquoise killifish, Nothobranchius furzeri, show the hallmarks of ageing, and can it serve as a short-timeframe model?

Approach. Characterisation of retina and optic tectum across four age groups, with markers of oxidative stress, gliosis and neurodegeneration. Female fish only.

Findings. Evidence of neurodegenerative change in the old retina, rising oxidative stress markers, gliosis, retinal thinning and functional vision loss. The strain lives six to eight months, so a full ageing study fits in under a year.

Why it matters. This is the permission slip for everything after it. Before you can claim a fish models an age-related disease, you must show the organ ages.

Limits, and an important dispute. Females only, and general markers rather than AMD-specific lesions. Then Bergmans and colleagues (2023, Biogerontology 24:403-419) argued that apparent thinning is confounded by tissue stretching as the eye keeps growing, not necessarily neurodegeneration. This is the model's most serious methodological objection, and you should carry it into any thinning result you read. The paper below answers it from a different direction.

6.6 Killifish molecules: what ageing does to the transcriptome

Bergmans S, Noel NCL, Masin L, Harding EG, Krzywańska AM, De Schutter JD, Ayana R, Hu CK, Arckens L, Ruzycki PA, MacDonald RB, Clark BS, Moons L (2024). Age-related dysregulation of the retinal transcriptome in African turquoise killifish. Aging Cell 23:e14192.

Question. Histology says the old killifish retina looks degenerate, and a critic says it may just be stretched. What do the genes say? Which pathways change with age, in which cell types, and do they overlap with human retinal disease?

Approach. Bulk RNA sequencing plus single-cell RNA sequencing of killifish retina at three ages, 6, 12 and 18 weeks, then integration of the two datasets at matched ages. Note the three timepoints, finer than the two used in the 2025 histology paper.

Findings.

  • Transcriptional ageing fingerprints matching those of the aged brain: oxidative stress, gliosis and inflammageing. These were comparable to what is seen in the ageing mouse retina, which is a useful cross-species check.
  • Changed expression of genes tied to human retinal disease, including glaucoma and AMD.
  • All typical vertebrate retinal cell types are present in the killifish retina, established by the single-cell data. Another piece of groundwork that licenses comparison.
  • A loss of cellular specificity in gene expression with age: cells switch on genes not normally characteristic of their type, suggesting the breakdown of transcriptional homeostasis rather than simple up or down regulation.
  • Glial and immune cells stood out as the main stress regulators during ageing.

Why it matters. It answers the stretching objection on ground where stretching cannot explain anything: a stretched tissue does not rewrite its transcriptome, and it certainly does not make cells express the wrong genes for their type. It also supplies the molecular target list that the later histology work follows up, apoeb and htra1b among them, and it is where the claim that killifish retinal cell types are conserved actually comes from.

Limits. Transcript levels are not protein levels and not lesions; this paper shows dysregulation, not damage. Its oldest group, 18 weeks, is younger than the 24-week animals in which deposits are most obvious, so the two studies do not line up exactly. And expression changes alone cannot say which changes are causes and which are consequences.

Pair 6.5 and 6.6 in your head. Vanhunsel looked at the tissue and said it degenerates. Bergmans 2023 said the tissue might only be stretched. Bergmans 2024 looked at the transcripts, where stretching has no explanatory power, and found genuine age-related dysregulation. That is how a methodological objection gets answered properly: not by arguing, but by changing the measurement.

6.7 The central paper: killifish develop AMD hallmarks on their own

Noel NCL, Breitenbach E-M, MacDonald RB (2025). Turquoise killifish naturally develop hallmarks of age-related macular degeneration with advancing age. bioRxiv, DOI 10.1101/2025.10.23.683644. Institute of Ophthalmology, UCL.

Status. A preprint, posted October 2025, not peer reviewed. Cite it as a preprint, and treat the numbers as provisional. The design and the reasoning are worth studying regardless, and the first author is the same person who built the zebrafish rp1l1 model in 6.3, so the two papers read as one line of thought.

The question

Existing AMD models either lack the right retina (rodents) or lack ageing (mutants, laser injury, cell culture). Killifish have a cone-rich retina and a six-to-eight-month lifespan. Does their retina spontaneously develop the specific hallmark lesions of AMD, without any genetic manipulation? And does sex matter, given that all previous killifish retina work used females?

Design

  • GRZ strain Nothobranchius furzeri, the short-lived laboratory strain.
  • Two ages: "young" at 6 weeks post-hatching (young adult) and "old" at 24 weeks (end of lifespan). Deposits were also checked at 18 weeks.
  • Both sexes, analysed separately. This is the design choice that produced the paper's most novel result.
  • Central versus peripheral retina compared, using the growth gradient from section 1 as an internal age control.
  • Methods: in situ hybridisation chain reaction for gene expression and localisation; histology with measurement of inner and outer segment length and outer segment width; immunofluorescence for opsin (zpr-3), PRPH2, oxidised phospholipids, ceramide and the macrophage marker L-plastin; neutral lipid staining with LipidTox and oil red O.

Findings, in the order the paper builds them

  1. The genes are in the right cells. Young retinas expressed rpe65a in the RPE and rp1l1, prph2b, elovl4b and eys in photoreceptors, all localised as expected from other species. Old retinas expressed them in the same cell types. This is groundwork, not a result: it licenses the comparison by showing the tissue is built the way human retina is built.
  2. Photoreceptors shorten. Old males had significantly shorter inner and outer segment regions than young fish. Old females trended shorter without reaching significance. Outer segment width did not differ between any group, which is the control that matters: the tissue is losing material rather than changing shape.
  3. Outer segment proteins end up in the wrong place. In old fish, zpr-3 and PRPH2 labelling appeared in inner segments, cell bodies and synaptic terminals, in cells with no identifiable outer segment at all. Protein that belongs in the outer segment accumulating in the cell body is a recognised signature of photoreceptor degeneration, and it is strong evidence because mislocalisation can itself kill the cell.
  4. Deposits form spontaneously. Lipid deposits appeared mainly in the central retina, few in the periphery, consistent with the age gradient. Most sat between outer segments and the RPE, but some were sub-RPE. They stained positive for oxidised phospholipids, which is the Handa criterion from 6.2. Opsin labelled with zpr-3 was found within or around the deposits.
  5. Males are affected earlier and worse. Males averaged more than four times as many central-retina deposits as females, and deposits were detectable by 18 weeks in males. Deposit composition was also more varied in males, some staining for neutral lipid only.
  6. The RPE is stressed. Ceramide, which signals stress and can trigger RPE death, was confined to the neural retina and Müller glia in young fish, but accumulated in the RPE of old fish, unevenly between neighbouring cells.
  7. Immune cells move in. Young fish had few L-plastin-positive macrophages near the outer segments, with small round cell bodies. Old fish had macrophages throughout the outer segment region, some apparently wrapping around outer segments, with thick processes and a motile appearance. Old males had significantly more than young fish or than their own peripheral retina.

The interpretation, and why it is the interesting part

Compare this with 6.4: there a metabolic blockage filled the RPE with retinoid droplets from the inside, with healthy photoreceptors above. Here the deposits are extracellular and carry photoreceptor protein. Two different mechanisms, two different lesions, both called lipid deposits in an abstract.

The deposits do not fit either human category cleanly. They are lipid-rich and oxidised-phospholipid-positive, which is drusen-like composition, but they sit between photoreceptors and RPE, which is subretinal drusenoid position. The authors call them hybrid, and propose that the killifish may form deposits on both sides of the RPE because it has abundant rods and cones rather than a rod-dominated retina. Separately, they note that killifish can also form genuinely sub-RPE lipid-rich deposits.

On how deposits arise, they offer two mutually exclusive hypotheses and are explicit that the data do not choose between them. Either outer-segment deterioration and RPE dysfunction leave shed material unphagocytosed, so it accumulates; or a degenerating RPE expels its own contents, such as opsin-containing phagosomes, into the subretinal space where they break down. The opsin found in and around deposits is consistent with both. Likewise, whether RPE dysfunction precedes deposit formation or follows it is left open as future work.

Learn the paper by its chain, not its figures: genes in the right cells, segments shorten, protein mislocalises, deposits appear, RPE stresses, macrophages arrive. Each link is one hallmark, and each is measured separately.

Limits, stated and unstated

  • A preprint without peer review.
  • Genetics was not tested. The authors say so, and name introducing human AMD variants, and high-fat or hypoxic challenge, as next steps.
  • Two timepoints only, 6 and 24 weeks (plus an 18-week deposit check), so the trajectory between them is inferred.
  • No functional vision measurement in this paper; damage is structural and molecular.
  • Still no macula, so "AMD-like" means cone-rich-retina pathology, not macular pathology.
  • Complement was not examined, despite being central to human disease and to both approved atrophy drugs. Given fish complement genes do not map one-to-one onto human ones, this is the hardest gap to close.
  • The sex difference is reported, not explained. The authors suggest hormones or metabolism, and note the human literature is itself inconsistent: some studies find late disease more common in women, possibly confounded by longer life expectancy, while another found large drusen and pigment change more likely in men.

Read as a set, they make one argument. Spaide says what counts as a deposit. Handa says why an oxidised deposit is dangerous. Noel 2020 shows a photoreceptor defect is sufficient to make one, and Schlegel shows an RPE metabolic blockage makes a different kind, so lipid accumulation has at least two independent routes. Vanhunsel shows the killifish eye genuinely ages, and Bergmans 2024 confirms it in the transcriptome where the stretching objection cannot reach. Noel 2025 then shows ageing alone, with no mutation, produces deposits plus three further hallmarks, and that sex changes the severity.

The shape of the argument. Notice that no single paper here is decisive, and the authors of each would say so. What carries the case is that the engineered models supply mechanism without ageing, the ageing models supply ageing without engineered mechanism, and they converge on the same lesions. Learning to see that convergence, rather than grading each paper alone, is most of what reading a literature means.

7. Reading a model paper: a checklist you can apply to any of them

The skill being built here is not recall of seven papers, it is the ability to read the eighth. Run any animal-model paper through these questions.

The question that survives all of this. Does the model let you do an experiment you could not otherwise do? Killifish let you watch deposits appear from scratch, in a cone-rich retina, in under six months, in an animal you can sample at will. No other system offers that, and that is the claim to judge, not whether a fish has a macula.
