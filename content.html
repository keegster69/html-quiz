Amino acids

Every amino acid = central α-carbon + amino group (–NH2) + carboxyl group (–COOH) + hydrogen + R group (side chain). Only the R group differs.

Zwitterion, in words: at pH 7 the α-amino group acts as a base and picks up a proton (–NH₃⁺, positive), while the α-carboxyl acts as an acid and loses one (–COO⁻, negative). Chirality: the α-carbon carries four different groups, so every amino acid except glycine is chiral. L and D forms are mirror images — they differ only in how –COOH, –NH₂, –H and R are arranged around the α-carbon — and proteins are built exclusively from L-amino acids. Hydrophobicity runs nonpolar > polar uncharged > charged, so for example Leu > Ser > Glu.

Peptide bond

A peptide bond is covalent, joins the carboxyl carbon of one residue to the amino nitrogen of the next, and forms by condensation (a water is removed). All three statements are true at once, which is how exam questions usually phrase it.

The genetic code

  • Triplet: 3 mRNA bases = 1 codon = 1 amino acid. 4³ = 64 codons.
  • Degenerate/redundant: 61 sense codons for 20 amino acids; most redundancy at the 3rd base (wobble).
  • Unambiguous: one codon never codes for two amino acids.
  • Nearly universal across all organisms (mitochondria are the exception).
  • Non-overlapping, read continuously from a fixed start, so insertions or deletions cause frameshifts.
  • AUG = start (Met; fMet in bacteria). UAA, UAG, UGA = stop (no tRNA; release factors bind).
  • Watch the direction. Codons are read 5'→3'. If a sequence is written 3'→5', reverse it before translating — e.g. 3'-AGGAGGAUGGUUUCGUAAA-5' is 5'-AAAUGCUUUGGUAGGAGGA-3', which reads AUG·CUU·UGG·UAG = Met–Leu–Trp–stop.
Mature tRNA contains more than the four standard bases. After transcription, tRNA is heavily processed and its bases are chemically modified: inosine (I, from adenosine — it sits in the anticodon wobble position and can pair with U, C or A), pseudouridine (Ψ), dihydrouridine and ribothymidine among others. So a mature tRNA does carry bases other than A, G, C and U — while DNA in the body sticks to A, G, C and T (uracil in DNA is damage, and is excised).

Central dogma

Direction rules: nucleic acids are always synthesized 5' → 3'; the template is read 3' → 5'. Proteins are synthesized N → C.

All polymers are built by dehydration synthesis, also called condensation (removes H₂O), and broken by hydrolysis (adds H₂O).

Naming and detail: two sugars joined by a glycosidic bond make a disaccharide (maltose, sucrose, lactose). The base is joined to the 1' carbon of the sugar by an N-glycosidic bond. Sugar + nitrogenous base = a nucleoside; add one or more phosphates and it is a nucleotide. So adenine + ribose = adenosine (a ribonucleoside); add three phosphates and it is ATP (a nucleotide); swap in deoxyribose and it is deoxyadenosine. Polymerases build nucleic acids from nucleoside triphosphates (NTPs for RNA, dNTPs for DNA), releasing pyrophosphate as each one is added to the free 3'-OH. Cellulose and starch are both glucose polymers, and the difference is the stereochemistry of the glycosidic linkage: β-1,4 gives straight, hydrogen-bonded, indigestible fibres, while α-1,4 gives a helical, digestible polymer. (α- and β-glucose are the same sugar differing only in the orientation of one hydroxyl on the ring; animals carry the enzymes for α-1,4 links but not β-1,4, which is why we digest starch and not cellulose.) A nucleic acid strand has 5'→3' polarity: the two ends are chemically different, one carrying a free 5' phosphate and the other a free 3'-OH, and synthesis only ever adds to that 3'-OH.

Lipids and sugars, a few more details: a triglyceride is glycerol + three fatty acids; swap one fatty acid for a phosphate head group and it is a phospholipid. Steroids (cholesterol) are four fused rings, not chains; cholesterol wedges between phospholipids and moderates fluidity — it stiffens a warm membrane and stops a cold one packing solid. Membranes are a fluid mosaic because lipids and many proteins diffuse laterally in the plane of the sheet while rarely flipping between leaflets. Chitin is a glucose polymer whose units carry nitrogen (N-acetylglucosamine). A glycoprotein is a protein with covalently attached carbohydrate, common on secreted and cell-surface proteins. A polysaccharide of one repeated sugar cannot store information; nucleic acids can because four distinguishable subunits sit in a defined sequence.

Bonds and non-covalent forces

  • C, H, N and O make up about 99% of the atoms in a cell. Chemistry is decided by the electrons, specifically the valence (outer-shell) ones: sodium 2311Na has 11 electrons — 2, 8, then 1 in the outer shell — so it forms one bond or loses one electron.
  • Isotopes differ only in neutron count: 18O has two more neutrons than 16O, same 8 protons, same chemistry — which is what makes isotope labelling possible.
  • Ions form when an electron moves across: Na hands its valence electron to Cl, leaving Na⁺ (more protons than electrons) and Cl⁻. The two then attract electrostatically.
  • Bond length is a property of the two atoms involved, and settles where attraction and repulsion balance. Push closer and repulsion rises steeply.
  • Strength ranking in cells: covalent > ionic > hydrogen > van der Waals. A hydrogen bond needs a hydrogen already in a polar covalent bond (on N, O or F) plus a second electronegative atom to accept it — it is non-covalent.
  • Water self-ionizes: 2 H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + OH⁻. Each pH unit is a factor of ten, so pH 5 has 100× the H⁺ of pH 7.
  • “Organic” in chemistry means carbon-based, nothing to do with farming: sugar is organic, water and sea salt are not.

Water: polar + H-bonding → cohesion/adhesion, high specific heat, high heat of vaporization, ice floats (less dense), universal solvent for polar/ionic solutes. Weak bonds are individually breakable but collectively decisive. That is what makes binding specific and reversible.

Why water behaves as it does: each molecule has two hydrogens to donate and two lone pairs on oxygen to accept, so it can make up to four hydrogen bonds at once. That network is what gives water its high boiling point and specific heat, its open (less dense) ice lattice, and its ability to dissolve ions: the partial charges of several water molecules orient around an ion as a hydration shell, an ion–dipole interaction. Heating breaks hydrogen bonds faster than they reform, which weakens the network. A molecule covered in hydroxyl groups donates and accepts hydrogen bonds, so it is hydrophilic.

pH, pKa and buffers: at pH = pKa a group is half protonated; every pH unit above the pKa leaves it ten times more deprotonated (2 units above ≈ 99% deprotonated), and below the pKa it is protonated. A buffer is a weak acid together with its conjugate base, and it resists pH change best within about 1 unit of the pKa, where both forms are plentiful. The pH scale is logarithmic, so pH 2 holds a thousand times the proton concentration of pH 5. Blood is held near pH 7.4 by the carbonic acid / bicarbonate pair, which interconverts to soak up added acid or base. At pH 7 a carboxyl group (pKa ≈ 4) is deprotonated and negative, while an amino group (pKa ≈ 10) is protonated and positive.

Coiled-coil: an α-helix whose sequence puts nonpolar residues every three or four positions shows a hydrophobic stripe running along one face (an amphipathic helix). Two or more such helices wind around each other so the stripes are buried away from water — the coiled-coil motif of keratin, myosin, and many nuclear-transport and structural proteins.

Protein families: a family is a set of proteins with a common evolutionary origin, recognizable from shared sequence and shared domains, with related — not necessarily identical — functions. Members need not share a quaternary structure or an exact sequence.

Purifying a protein: affinity chromatography holds the protein on a matrix that binds it specifically; stepping up the salt concentration screens charges and competes for ionic (electrostatic) contacts, so the protein elutes in a known fraction. Ion-exchange separates by charge, gel filtration by size, and SDS-PAGE reports size on a gel.

Also worth knowing: a domain is a compact, independently folding region of a chain that usually has its own function and recurs across many proteins. Integral membrane proteins cross the bilayer through stretches rich in hydrophobic residues that match the lipid interior. A mutation in the tightly packed hydrophobic core destabilizes far more than one on the surface, because the core has no room for a charged or bulky replacement. Molecular chaperones raise folding yield by binding exposed hydrophobic surfaces and preventing aggregation — the sequence still specifies the final structure (Anfinsen). Atomic-resolution structures come from X-ray crystallography (and cryo-EM / NMR).

Denaturation (heat, extreme pH, urea, detergents, reducing agents) breaks weak bonds and unfolds the protein. Primary structure survives, function does not. Often reversible if the sequence is intact. Structure determines function is the recurring exam point.

Enzymes, reactions and ATP

Enzyme basics

  • Biological catalyst that lowers activation energy (Ea), speeds the reaction, is not consumed and does not change ΔG or equilibrium.
  • Active site binds substrate; induced fit means the site reshapes around the substrate.
  • Specific for substrate and reaction. Named for what they act on: -ase.
  • Sensitive to temperature and pH; beyond the optimum, denaturation kills activity.
  • Cofactors = inorganic (Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺); coenzymes = organic (NAD⁺, FAD, coenzyme A, often from vitamins).
  • Kinetics: Vmax is reached when every active site is saturated with substrate; Km is the substrate concentration giving half Vmax, so a low Km means high affinity. A competitive inhibitor raises apparent Km and leaves Vmax unchanged; a noncompetitive one lowers Vmax.
  • Michaelis–Menten relates velocity to substrate concentration through Km and Vmax, giving a hyperbola. The Lineweaver–Burk double-reciprocal plot straightens it so Km and Vmax can be read off the intercepts and inhibitor types told apart. Sigmoidal (S-shaped) kinetics instead signal cooperative binding between subunits, the hallmark of an allosterically regulated enzyme.
  • Zymogens (pepsinogen, trypsinogen) are inactive precursors switched on by cleavage, which keeps digestive enzymes from destroying the cell that makes them.
  • Favourable ≠ fast. A negative ΔG says nothing about rate: a large activation-energy barrier keeps a favourable reaction immeasurably slow until an enzyme lowers it by stabilizing the transition state. An enzyme cannot make a positive-ΔG reaction go; only coupling it to a strongly exergonic one (ATP hydrolysis) can, by making the summed ΔG negative.

Types of organisms and cell composition

  • What a cell is made of: about 70% water by mass, so water is the most abundant molecule. Of the dry weight, protein is roughly 50% — the most abundant macromolecule. C, H, O and N account for ~96% of the atoms; P and S are essential but minor.
  • Three domains: Woese compared small-subunit rRNA sequences and found archaea are as distinct from bacteria as they are from eukaryotes. Every cell, in all three domains, translates mRNA on ribosomes; nuclei, mitochondria and histones are eukaryote features.
  • No nucleus + a cell wall + one circular chromosome tells you only that a cell is prokaryotic. Separating bacteria from archaea needs membrane-lipid chemistry or sequence data.
  • Archaea vs bacteria, chemically: archaeal membranes use ether-linked lipids and their walls have no peptidoglycan; bacteria use ester-linked lipids and peptidoglycan walls.
  • One genome, many cell types: every cell of a multicellular organism carries the same DNA, so differentiation comes from differential gene expression — which in turn needs cell-to-cell signalling and coordinated regulation that a single-celled organism can do without.
  • Why E. coli: the standard model organism because it divides fast (~20 min), is cheap and easy to culture, and is readily manipulated genetically (plasmids, phage, mutant collections). Yeast is the next step up: a single-celled eukaryote with a nucleus and organelles, so eukaryotic cell biology can be studied with microbial-scale genetics and screens.

The “omics” layers: the genome is all the DNA, the transcriptome is everything actually transcribed in a given cell, and the proteome is the proteins present. Comparing genome with transcriptome is how whole classes of non-coding RNAs were found: they are transcribed but never appear in the proteome.

Where eukaryotes came from: beyond endosymbiosis for the organelles, the inside-out model proposes that an archaeal cell extended protrusions around its bacterial partner; fusing those protrusions would generate the nuclear envelope and the ER in one stroke. It is an explanatory hypothesis, not a settled account.

The 70S/80S difference is why several antibiotics (tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, macrolides) block bacterial translation without touching the host’s own ribosomes. The same logic picks the best drug target in general: hit something bacteria have and we do not — blocking Shine–Dalgarno pairing with 16S rRNA stops bacterial initiation, while eukaryotic ribosomes scan from a cap instead and are untouched.

Every cell, without exception, has a chromosome (DNA), ribosomes and a plasma membrane; cell walls, nuclei, chloroplasts and organelles are all optional extras. Robert Hooke built the first useful compound microscope and named the “cell” in 1665. Three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya. Cell theory: all organisms are made of cells; the cell is the basic unit of life; cells come only from pre-existing cells. Viruses are acellular, so they are not alive by these criteria. Elements by mass: C, H, O, N account for ~96% of living matter.

DNA structure and replication

  • Double helix, antiparallel strands, sugar-phosphate backbone outside, bases inside.
  • Backbone joined by phosphodiester bonds (5' phosphate to 3' OH); negatively charged → runs to the + electrode in a gel.
  • Replication is semiconservative: each daughter duplex = 1 old + 1 new strand.
  • DNA polymerase can only add to a free 3'-OH → synthesis is strictly 5'→3' and a primer is mandatory.
  • B-DNA makes a full turn every ~10 base pairs (3.4 nm), leaving a wide major groove and a narrow minor groove; sequence-specific binding proteins read the edges of the bases, mostly in the major groove.
  • Melting is reversible: heat separates the strands, and slow cooling lets complementary strands renature (reanneal) — the principle behind probing, blotting and PCR. Strand separation can be followed as a hyperchromic shift, a rise in absorbance at 260 nm as the bases unstack.
  • Eukaryotic DNA is wrapped with histones: ~147 bp around a histone octamer makes one nucleosome, the repeating packaging unit. Supercoiling is the helix coiling on itself, set and relieved by topoisomerases.
  • Replication starts at a defined origin and is bidirectional: two forks move apart, making a replication bubble. Bacteria use one origin; eukaryotes fire many in parallel, because the genomes are far larger and their polymerases slower.
  • Fidelity is layered: base selection, then 3'→5' proofreading, then mismatch repair, which in bacteria tells the new strand from the template because the daughter strand is transiently unmethylated. Together they reach roughly one error per billion bases; losing proofreading raises the mutation rate by orders of magnitude.

Chromatin packing

  • Least → most compact: DNA double helix → “beads on a string” nucleosomes → 30 nm fibre → chromatin looped onto a scaffold → the condensed mitotic chromosome.
  • A nucleosome core is two each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4 with ~147 bp wrapped round it; counting the linker, one nucleosome repeat ≈ 200 bp, so a stretch of 10 nucleosomes is about 2,000 bp.
  • Histones are rich in lysine and arginine, positively charged at cell pH, which is what grips the negatively charged phosphate backbone. Proteins that bind RNA are often basic for exactly the same reason.
  • Euchromatin is open; heterochromatin is compact and largely silent. Ranked by how likely they are to be transcribed: active euchromatin > quiescent euchromatin > facultative heterochromatin (silenced in this cell but not always) > constitutive heterochromatin (permanently packed, e.g. centromeres and telomeres).
  • For most of interphase chromosomes are decondensed tangled threads, not individually visible by light microscopy; they only resolve once condensed for mitosis.

Genome counting: a “genome” is one haploid set. A human diploid cell with 46 chromosomes therefore carries two genomes, one from each parent (the amount of DNA also depends on cell-cycle stage). The mitochondrial genome is tiny — ~16.6 kb against E. coli’s ~4.6 Mb — because most genes of the bacterial ancestor were lost or transferred to the nucleus; the nucleus now encodes most mitochondrial proteins, which are made in the cytosol and imported.

Two details about the fork: the main replicative helicase in bacteria (DnaB) travels 5'→3' along the lagging-strand template, while the eukaryotic CMG helicase moves 3'→5' on the leading-strand template. And a proofreading DNA polymerase has two separate active sites in one enzyme: the polymerizing site and the 3'→5' exonuclease site, with the mispaired end shuttling between them.

Historical note: before the 1940s proteins were the favoured candidate for the genetic material, because 20 different amino acids looked capable of carrying complex information while DNA’s four bases seemed monotonous. Chargaff’s varying base ratios killed that tetranucleotide hypothesis; Hershey and Chase’s blender mattered because it sheared the empty phage coats off the cells so the two labels could be separated. Without telomerase, ends shorten every division, which acts as a replicative clock and drives senescence.

Partial diploids (merodiploids, usually made with an F′ plasmid) are the classic cis/trans test: a site that only controls genes on its own molecule is cis-acting (operator, promoter), while a gene whose product diffuses through the cytoplasm is trans-acting (lacI).

Jacob and Monod built the operon model exactly this way, from mutant phenotypes in partial diploids: whichever elements could be rescued by a copy elsewhere in the cell were diffusible (trans), and the rest acted only where they sat (cis). Even when repressed the operon is slightly leaky, because the repressor occasionally falls off the operator — and it has to be, since the cell needs a few permease and β-galactosidase molecules present to notice lactose at all. A mutation in the promoter that weakens polymerase binding lowers transcription even under full induction, because the promoter sets the ceiling.

Inducible vs repressible: lac is inducible — the substrate switches it on. trp is repressible — the end product tryptophan acts as a corepressor, activating its repressor to switch the operon off when tryptophan is plentiful. Catabolic pathways are usually inducible, biosynthetic ones repressible. Ignoring lactose while glucose is around is worth it because glucose is the cheaper fuel, so the lactose enzymes would be wasted protein.

Grouping lacZ, lacY and lacA in one operon means a single polycistronic mRNA, so the whole lactose-utilization system is switched on and off together.

Overall: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O. Label every carbon of glucose with 14C and the label comes out in CO₂; the water is made at the end of the electron transport chain, where oxygen accepts the electrons. Pyruvate made in the cytosol is carried into the matrix and oxidatively decarboxylated there by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex; coenzyme A picks up and activates the resulting two-carbon acetyl group for entry into the TCA cycle. After glycolysis and the cycle the carbon is gone as CO₂ but most of the energy is still held as NADH, which is why blocking the chain strands it there. Fermentation earns no extra ATP: it hands electrons to an organic acceptor purely to regenerate NAD⁺, which keeps glycolysis running and lets tissues work while oxygen is short.

Why the chain runs the way it does: the complexes are ordered by increasing electron affinity, so electrons fall stepwise and release energy in usable amounts; that energy is stored first as a proton-motive force, which is both a pH gradient and a membrane voltage, before ATP synthase spends it. Blocking ATP synthase while the chain still runs lets the gradient build until back-pressure slows electron transport — the two are coupled. NADH donates at complex I; FADH₂ enters at complex II, skipping one pumping site, which is why it yields less ATP.

Mendel's laws and pedigrees

  • Law of segregation: the two alleles of a gene separate during meiosis; each gamete gets one.
  • Law of independent assortment: genes on different chromosomes assort independently.
  • Dominance: the dominant allele masks the recessive in a heterozygote.
  • Genotype = alleles carried; phenotype = trait shown. Homozygous (AA/aa) vs heterozygous (Aa).

Probability rules: for independent events, multiply (product rule). So aabb from AaBb × AaBb = ¼ × ¼ = 1/16, and an AaBb parent makes 2ⁿ = 4 gamete types (AB, Ab, aB, ab) in equal proportions. Each conception is independent of the ones before it.

Beyond simple dominance: ABO blood type shows multiple alleles in a population (three) with A and B codominant. Pleiotropy is one gene affecting several unrelated traits (the sickle allele). Polygenic traits such as height come from many genes of small effect plus environment, giving continuous bell-shaped variation instead of discrete classes. A recessive lethal turns the 3:1 into a 2:1 ratio among survivors, since the homozygous class dies; a dominant lethal acting before reproduction is eliminated almost at once. Mendel was ignored in his lifetime partly because his statistical, particulate model clashed with the prevailing idea of blending inheritance; he chose peas because he could control pollination and follow clearly contrasting traits in true-breeding lines.

Linkage, recombination and maps

  • Genes on the same chromosome are linked and violate independent assortment, so parental combinations outnumber recombinants.
  • Crossing over in prophase I of meiosis breaks linkage; the closer two genes are, the less often it happens.
Recombination frequency (RF) = (recombinant offspring / total offspring) × 100. 1% RF = 1 map unit = 1 centimorgan (cM). RF maxes out at 50%, which means unlinked (or far apart on the same chromosome).

Where this came from: Morgan’s work on Drosophila tied a gene (white eyes) to a specific chromosome and showed linked genes still recombine; Sturtevant then turned recombination frequencies into the first genetic map. Flies suit the job because of short generations, huge broods and few chromosomes — and crossing over does not happen in male Drosophila at all, a quirk used in mapping crosses. Map distance and physical distance agree only roughly, because recombination is suppressed near centromeres and elevated at hotspots. The most informative cross for linkage is a test cross of a dihybrid to a homozygous recessive, since the recessive parent contributes nothing but recessive alleles and the offspring phenotypes read out the dihybrid’s gametes directly.

An expression library puts cDNA behind a bacterial promoter so protein is made. Screen it with an antibody instead of a nucleic-acid probe. For that to work the insert must sit downstream of the promoter, in the right orientation and the right reading frame — only one of six arrangements does, which is why so many clones must be screened.

How cDNA is actually made: oligo-dT primes on the poly-A tail and reverse transcriptase copies the mRNA into the first DNA strand; the RNA is then nicked away (RNase H) and a polymerase builds the second strand. Because priming starts at the 3' end, an enzyme that falls off early truncates the 5' end — the usual reason a cDNA clone is incomplete, and why a full-length clone is worth having. A rare transcript is rare in the library too, so low-abundance genes mean many more clones to screen. Aligning a cDNA against its genomic sequence maps the intron/exon structure directly. Protein made in bacteria can still misbehave: no eukaryotic modifications such as glycosylation, and over-expression often dumps it into inclusion bodies (insoluble misfolded aggregates), fixed by lowering temperature or expression level. An affinity tag (His tag) lets the product be purified in one specific binding-and-elution step.

Building and screening a library

  • Partial digest: limit the reaction so only some sites are cut. That gives large, randomly overlapping fragments covering the genome without gaps; a complete digest would cut every copy at the same places and destroy anything spanning a site.
  • Complexity: the number of independent clones needed for a high probability that every sequence is represented. It rises with genome size and falls with insert size, and a low-complexity library is simply missing sequences.
  • Colony hybridization: the colonies themselves are lifted onto a filter, their DNA is denatured and bound in place, and a labeled probe finds the matching clone by complementary base pairing.
  • Stringency: raising the wash temperature or lowering the salt raises stringency, so only closely matched duplexes survive. Lower stringency tolerates mismatches and can pull out related genes from other species.
  • Only know the protein sequence? Work backwards through the code. Because it is degenerate, several DNA sequences could encode the peptide, so you synthesize a mixture of degenerate oligonucleotides covering the possible codons.
  • Genome equivalents: clones needed for one equivalent = genome size ÷ insert size (3 × 10⁹ bp ÷ 20 kb ≈ 150,000). Because sampling is random, one equivalent leaves real gaps, so several-fold coverage is screened. Libraries are also aliquoted and re-grown as little as possible, since slow-growing clones drop out and skew representation.
  • Plaque hybridization is the phage version of colony hybridization — each plaque on the lawn is one clone, lifted onto a filter the same way. Duplicate lifts are taken because a signal appearing on both is far more likely to be real than a one-filter artifact. A positive on the filter is a map back to the plate: pick that colony or plaque and purify it.
  • Chromosome walking: use the end of a known clone as the probe to pull out the next overlapping clone, and repeat, to reach an adjacent region. Genes with very large introns often span several clones and have to be assembled this way.
  • Subtractive hybridization: remove the sequences two samples share so the ones unique to one sample are enriched — a way to find tissue-specific transcripts.
  • Screen gives no positives? Real failure modes are a bad probe, too few clones for the complexity needed, or hybridization conditions that are too stringent — not the gene being present, which is the assumption you started from.

Membranes and transport

  • Fluid mosaic: phospholipids and many proteins diffuse laterally within a leaflet, and transmembrane proteins keep their orientation while they do. Spontaneous flip-flop between leaflets is rare (it needs flippase enzymes), which is what lets the two leaflets stay different.
  • The two leaflets are not the same. They carry different phospholipids, and all the carbohydrate — the glycolipids and glycoprotein sugars — faces outward, never into the cytosol.
  • Fluidity: more unsaturated fatty acids (kinked tails, poor packing) = more fluid, so cold-adapted organisms raise their unsaturated fraction. Shorter tails do the same. Cholesterol buffers both ways: it stiffens a warm membrane and stops a cold one packing solid.
  • Transmembrane proteins stay put because of where their residues sit: a nonpolar stretch spans the bilayer interior while polar and charged residues anchor at the two aqueous faces.
Route acrossHow it works
Simple diffusionSmall nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂) cross the bilayer unaided, down their gradient.
ChannelA gated pore. Ions flow down their electrochemical gradient at very high rates, and a channel does not saturate while the gradient lasts.
Carrier (transporter)Binds its solute and changes conformation, so it is slower and saturable — raise the cargo concentration enough and every carrier is occupied, exactly like an enzyme at Vmax.
Primary active transportUses ATP directly to pump against a gradient — the Na⁺/K⁺ pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in per ATP).
Secondary active transportUses a gradient someone else paid for. Intestinal glucose uptake is a Na⁺/glucose symport: glucose rides in against its own gradient on the back of the Na⁺ gradient that the Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains. An antiport runs the two solutes in opposite directions.
Osmosis: water moves toward the higher solute concentration. An animal cell in a hypotonic solution swells and can burst; a bacterial or plant cell does not, because its cell wall resists the turgor pressure. Hypertonic shrinks a cell, isotonic leaves it unchanged.

Protein sorting and the endomembrane system

  • Default is the cytosol. Every protein starts on a ribosome in the cytosol; it goes somewhere else only if it carries a signal (localization) sequence. Delete the ER signal sequence and the protein stays in the cytosol. A purely cytosolic enzyme such as phosphofructokinase has no localization sequence at all, and so are RNA polymerases made — on free ribosomes in the cytosol, then imported into the nucleus.
  • Secretory pathway: rough ER → Golgi → plasma membrane (or lysosome), carried between compartments in vesicles. The ER is the largest component of the endomembrane system by membrane area.
  • Exocytosis fuses a vesicle with the plasma membrane: cargo goes out and the vesicle membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane. Endocytosis is the reverse, bringing material from outside in.
  • Topology is preserved. Whichever face of the ER membrane a protein domain protrudes from, it protrudes from the equivalent face all the way along: cytosolic face of the ER → cytosolic face of the Golgi → cytosolic face of the plasma membrane. The ER/Golgi lumen is topologically the outside of the cell, which is why sugars added on the luminal side end up on the extracellular surface.
  • Nuclear pore complexes let small molecules (and particles smaller than a small protein) diffuse through non-selectively, while anything large needs a nuclear localization signal and active transport. Gold nanoparticles injected into the cytoplasm turn up in the nucleus for exactly this reason.

Acetylcholine tells heart pacemaker cells to slow down, salivary gland cells to secrete, and skeletal muscle to contract. The signal is a doorbell. What happens when it rings depends on who lives in the house.

Signal transduction: switches, second messengers and kinases

Inside the cell the message is handed from protein to protein. Each hand-off changes the next protein's shape or activity, the signal gets bigger at several steps, and every step can be switched off again.

Two kinds of molecular switch

SwitchTurned on byTurned off by
PhosphorylationA protein kinase moves a phosphate from ATP onto the protein. Serine/threonine kinases (PKA, PKC, Cdks) or tyrosine kinases (RTKs).A protein phosphatase removes the phosphate.
GTP bindingThe protein swaps GDP for GTP, helped by a GEF (guanine nucleotide exchange factor).The protein hydrolyses its own GTP to GDP. A GAP (GTPase-activating protein) speeds that up.

A protein's phosphorylation state is a running balance between the kinases adding phosphate and the phosphatases taking it off, so it can change within seconds. Adding a phosphate turns some proteins on and others off; it depends on the protein.

In a phosphorylation cascade one kinase phosphorylates the next kinase, which phosphorylates the next. Each active enzyme switches on many copies of the one below it, so a handful of signal molecules can change thousands of target proteins. That is amplification.

GTP means Go, GDP means Done. Kinase adds phosphate, phosphatase takes it away.

How a GPCR switches on its G protein

  1. Ligand binds the GPCR
  2. Receptor changes shape
  3. Gα swaps GDP for GTP
  4. Gα-GTP separates from Gβγ
  5. Both parts activate targets
  6. Gα hydrolyses GTP and rejoins Gβγ

The trimeric G protein has α, β and γ subunits held to the inner face of the membrane by lipid tails. At rest α holds GDP and all three sit together. The activated receptor acts as the exchange factor. Because Gα hydrolyses its own GTP, the signal lasts only seconds unless the receptor keeps firing. Gβγ can signal too: in heart pacemaker cells the Gβγ released by the acetylcholine receptor opens K⁺ channels, which is why the heart slows.

A Ras that cannot hydrolyse GTP is a car with the accelerator taped to the floor.

  • G0 is a resting state outside the cycle. Some cells sit there for a while and come back (liver cells after injury). Terminally differentiated cells such as neurons and skeletal muscle fibres have left the cycle for good.

The first clue came from frog eggs. Cytoplasm from an egg in M phase, injected into an immature oocyte, drove it into M phase too. The active ingredient was called MPF (maturation- or M-phase-promoting factor), and it turned out to be a cyclin bound to a Cdk.

  2. Phosphates on the Cdk. Wee1 kinase adds an inhibitory phosphate; the Cdc25 phosphatase removes it. Removing it all at once is what fires M-Cdk and launches mitosis.

Cancer and apoptosis

Cancer cells divide when they should not and fail to die when they should. The cause is a pile-up of somatic mutations (in body cells, not passed to children), sometimes on top of an inherited weak gene. It usually takes several hits in the same cell lineage, which is why cancer risk climbs steeply with age. Once repair genes or checkpoints fail, the cell becomes genetically unstable and new mutations pile up faster still.

How cancer spreads

  • A benign tumour stays where it started, often inside a capsule. A malignant tumour invades the tissue around it.
  • Metastasis: cells lose their adhesion (fibronectin and cadherin contacts), break through the basal lamina, enter blood or lymph, and settle and grow somewhere else. Most cancer deaths are caused by metastases.
  • Typical cancer cells divide without mitogens, ignore contact with neighbours, escape apoptosis, keep telomeres long with telomerase, are genetically unstable, and recruit their own blood supply.

Caspases are proteases with a cysteine in the active site that cut their targets after an aspartate. They are made as inactive pro-caspases. One active caspase cuts and activates many more, so the proteolytic cascade amplifies itself and cannot be reversed. The Bcl-2 family decides whether it starts: anti-apoptotic members (Bcl-2 itself) guard the mitochondrion, and pro-apoptotic members (Bax, Bak) punch holes in it. The balance between the two sides decides the cell's fate.

Apoptosis sculpts the body (it removes the webbing between fingers), keeps cell numbers steady in adults, and removes damaged or infected cells. In the developing nervous system about half the neurons die: only those that reach their target and receive enough survival factor live, so the number of neurons ends up matched to the number of target cells.

Apoptosis is a tidy demolition with the rubble cleared away. Necrosis is an explosion. Caspase: a Cysteine protease that cuts after ASPartate.

Microtubules

  • They grow out from a microtubule-organizing centre (MTOC). In animal cells that is the centrosome: a pair of centrioles in a cloud of protein that contains γ-tubulin rings, where new microtubules start.
  • Tubulin dimers stack head to tail into protofilaments, so every protofilament, and the whole tube, has a direction. The minus end stays anchored at the centrosome; the plus end grows and shrinks out toward the cell edge.
  • Dynamic instability: a plus end keeps growing while it has a cap of GTP-tubulin. If it loses the cap it collapses quickly. Capping proteins that bind the end stabilize it.
  • Colchicine blocks tubulin from polymerizing and taxol freezes microtubules so they cannot shrink. Either way the spindle cannot work and dividing cells stall in mitosis, which is why taxol is used against cancer.

Actin microfilaments

  • Actin is the most abundant protein in most animal cells, 5 to 10% of all protein, and about half of it is in filaments at any moment. The rest is a pool of free monomers ready to build.
  • Actin does very different jobs in different places because of the many accessory proteins that bundle, cross-link, cap or cut the filaments.
  • Cell crawling happens in three steps. The front protrudes as actin polymerizes at the leading edge. The new front attaches to the surface through integrins. Myosin contracts and pulls the rear forward.

Intermediate filaments

Each monomer has a long central α-helical rod domain with globular ends. Two monomers coil into a dimer, and two dimers lie side by side pointing in opposite directions, so the finished rope has no polarity and no motor can walk on it. They make cells tough rather than mobile.

Cell junctions, extracellular matrix, tissues and stem cells

Plant cell walls

  • The primary wall is laid down while the cell is still growing. It is thin and can stretch: cellulose microfibrils embedded in hemicellulose and pectin.
  • The secondary wall is added inside the primary wall once growth stops. It is thicker and often stiffened with lignin, which is what wood is.
  • The middle lamella is a pectin-rich layer that glues the walls of neighbouring cells together. Pectin is the same stuff that sets jam.
  • Cellulose is β-1,4-linked glucose: straight chains that hydrogen-bond side by side into strong microfibrils. It is the most abundant organic molecule on Earth. Cellulose synthase complexes in the plasma membrane spin it out into the wall, running along tracks set by microtubules just under the membrane, so the microtubules decide which way the cell can expand.
  • Turgor pressure: water enters the cell by osmosis and presses the membrane against the wall, which pushes back. That is what keeps a plant upright. A wilting plant has lost turgor.
Cellulose is the steel rebar. Pectin is the glue between neighbours. Water pressure inflates the whole structure.

Animal tissues and their extracellular matrix

In connective tissue (bone, cartilage, tendon, skin dermis) the cells are sparse and the extracellular matrix (ECM) carries the load. In epithelia the cells are packed tightly and hold each other up through junctions, with only a thin sheet of ECM, the basal lamina, underneath.

Differentiation and stem cells

  • Differentiation: cells become specialised by expressing different sets of genes. They keep the same genome.
  • A stem cell can do two things: renew itself (divide and keep a stem cell) and give rise to differentiated cells.
  • Potency describes how many cell types a stem cell can make. Embryonic stem cells (from the inner cell mass) are pluripotent and can make any cell in the body. Hematopoietic stem cells in bone marrow are multipotent, making every kind of blood cell. A unipotent stem cell makes one cell type.
  • Tissues that wear out fast, like the gut lining, skin and blood, rely on stem cells to replace what is lost.
Stem cell = self-renewal plus making something else. Potency shrinks as development goes on: toti, pluri, multi, uni.
